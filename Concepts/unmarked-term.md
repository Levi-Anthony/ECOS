# Unmarked Term

The **unmarked term** in a linguistic pair or hierarchy is the form carrying no restrictive feature specification, and therefore capable of standing in for the whole category. In the pair *lion/lioness*, "lion" is unmarked — it names both the species and the male. In the pair *man/woman*, "man" has historically functioned as unmarked.

Unmarked terms appear to violate hierarchical placement: they seem like they should be at a specific level, but keep bleeding into other levels. This is a feature, not a bug — the absence of marking *is* the mechanism of generality. An unmarked term can substitute for the whole category because it has no feature that would exclude any member.

"Example" is the unmarked term of the example-typicality spectrum. Every other term in the hierarchy — paradigm, prototype, exemplar, specimen, instance — is a *marked* variant: it adds a specific constraint (institutional designation, statistical centrality, pedagogical utility, inert reference, logical neutrality) on top of the base concept. "Example" adds nothing, which is why it names the domain.

The relationship between markedness and ontological priority is an open question: do unmarked terms tend to name ontologically prior things? Suggestive evidence exists (the unmarked term often names the thing that exists independently of the contrast), but no formal account has been established here.
