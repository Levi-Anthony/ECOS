# Taxonomy vs Mereology

**Taxonomy** and **mereology** are the two fundamental types of hierarchical relation, and confusing them produces structural errors in how a hierarchy is interpreted.

A **taxonomic (is-a) relation** entails property inheritance: every subordinate instance inherits the properties of its superordinate. Substitution up the hierarchy preserves logical type — a robin is a bird, and what is true of birds is true of robins. Transitivity holds cleanly.

A **meronymic (part-of) relation** does not entail inheritance: parts do not inherit the properties of their wholes. A wheel is part of a car but is not a car. Substitution up the hierarchy changes what the thing fundamentally is. Transitivity is fragile — "a finger is part of a hand, a hand is part of a body" holds, but "a goose is part of a flock" does not make a goose's leg part of the flock.

Winston et al. identified six distinct subtypes of part-whole relation, differing in separability, homeomericity (whether the part is the same kind of stuff as the whole), and transitivity behavior: component–integral object, member–collection, portion–mass, stuff–object, feature–activity, place–area.

The distinction matters for hierarchy orientation analysis: authority-at-the-top hierarchies (military rank, legal standing) are closer to meronymic in spirit — lower ranks are *constituents* of the institution, not *types* of it — even when they superficially resemble taxonomies.
