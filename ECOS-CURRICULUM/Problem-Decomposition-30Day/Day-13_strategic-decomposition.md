# Day 13 — Strategic Decomposition: Where-to-Play / How-to-Win

> Week 2 · Practice

## Learning objective

Decompose a growth/strategy choice into **where to play** (which arena) and **how to win** (what
edge in that arena) — so the structure exposes which arena you can actually defend, not just which
sounds biggest.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a where-to-play / how-to-win options tree for the practice case).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

Strategy decomposition splits one big "how do we grow / where do we go?" into two linked questions.
**Where to play** is the arena: which program, market, segment, or offering you'd commit to. **How
to win** is the edge: the specific reason *you* would beat the alternatives in that arena — a
capability, a relationship, a cost position, a reputation. The two are inseparable. An arena with no
plausible edge is a trap; an edge with no arena to deploy it is a hobby.

The beginner failure is to enumerate *only* arenas — "we could grow beginners, or workshops, or
privates, or corporate gigs" — a tidy options list with no "how to win" attached, so you pick the one
that *sounds* biggest. The discipline: for every "where to play" branch, force a "how to win" answer.
The branches where you *can't* name a credible edge prune themselves. That's the whole point — most
of a strategy tree's value is in the branches it lets you *eliminate*, not the one it crowns.

Two extra pressures live here that the earlier weeks' problems didn't have. **Ambiguity:** the
payoffs are genuinely uncertain, so you reason about edge and fit, not just arithmetic.
**Irreversibility:** strategic commitments are expensive to unwind, so the cost of picking the
wrong arena is high — which raises the value of the structure that helped you reject the wrong ones.
This is also where **lens selection** starts to bite, which is exactly what Week 3 trains.

## Practice case

**The TTC board, where you sit, must set next year's festival direction** (Case-Bank #17,
enriched). Attendance and volunteer energy have been drifting down for two years. Three options are
on the table: (a) **go bigger** — a marquee out-of-town headliner to draw a regional crowd;
(b) **go deeper** — a smaller, members-focused festival built around community and workshops;
(c) **go adjacent** — add a fusion/alt-music night to attract a younger crowd. Messy details: the
budget covers one bet, not two; the board is split and a vocal member keeps pushing the headliner;
last year's headliner drew a crowd but few of them came back. **Distractor:** the headliner's
attendance spike — impressive, but it didn't convert to renewals. **Plausible-but-wrong lens:**
ranking the three options purely by projected attendance (where-to-play with no how-to-win) — picks
the marquee bet, which the board can't actually defend against bigger regional festivals.
**Why it's not obvious:** "biggest crowd" and "strongest edge" point at different options.
**Constraint:** the board votes at next month's meeting; you can't pilot all three or commission a
survey first.

Build the where-to-play / how-to-win tree, attach an edge to each arena, and name which option the
structure lets you reject.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A where-to-play / how-to-win options tree (3+ arenas, each with a named edge or an explicit "no
credible edge"), ending in a recommended arena and at least one rejected one, plus a one-line note on
the irreversibility risk.

**Definition of Done:** every "where to play" branch has a "how to win" answer (or is pruned for
lacking one); the recommendation rests on a defensible edge, not the biggest projected number; you can
name **which arena/edge would actually change the board's vote** and **which branch is tidy but
probably low-value** here (e.g., the marquee headliner — big number, no defensible edge); and you can
name the decision it improves — the festival-direction vote.

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] Every "where to play" arena has a "how to win" edge attached (or is pruned for lacking one).
- [ ] My recommendation rests on a defensible edge, not the largest projected number.
- [ ] I named the irreversibility/commitment risk of the chosen arena.
- [ ] **Which arena/edge would actually change the decision — and which branch is tidy but probably low-value?**
- [ ] **What evidence, example, or counterexample would show this structure is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant?**
- [ ] I can state the decision this structure improves (the board vote).

## Common mistake to watch for

**Enumerating arenas without edges** — a clean list of "where to play" options with no "how to win"
attached, ranked by which sounds biggest. The marquee headliner wins that ranking every time and
loses every real contest. No branch is a real strategy until it names why *you* would win there.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** for genuine strategic choices — which program to grow, whether to productize, what direction
to commit a scarce budget to. **Don't** use it for operational or diagnostic problems (a leaking
funnel wants Day 10's process map, a recurring failure wants Day 11's root cause); strategy
decomposition on a problem that's really execution just produces grand abstractions. And don't run it
without the irreversibility check — its whole payoff is helping you *not* commit to the wrong arena.

## Reflection question

Did attaching a "how to win" to each arena kill the option that *sounded* biggest? Notice how often
the most attractive arena is the one where you have the least defensible edge — and how the structure
is what made that visible.
