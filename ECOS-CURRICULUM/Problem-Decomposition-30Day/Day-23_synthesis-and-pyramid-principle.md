# Day 23 — Synthesis & the Pyramid Principle

> Week 4 · Synthesis & Communication

## Learning objective

Roll a pile of findings *up* into ONE governing message supported by three reasons (Minto's
pyramid) — and tell the difference between a synthesis and a data dump that merely lists what you
found.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a one-message-plus-three-supports pyramid for the attendance findings below).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

Analysis produces facts. **Synthesis answers "So what?"** — it states the single message those
facts add up to, then arranges the evidence *underneath* it as support. The pyramid principle
(Barbara Minto) is the shape: one governing message on top; 3 (±1) supporting reasons below it;
evidence under each reason. Read top-down, it's an argument; read bottom-up, it's how you built
it.

The discipline is **rolling up, not listing across.** A data dump says: "Attendance is down 40%.
The 6pm slot is the worst. Most empties live far away. New triers rarely return." Four true facts,
no message — the reader has to do the synthesis themselves, and they won't. A synthesis says:
"**The 6pm slot is failing on schedule fit, not on quality — so the move is to change the time,
not the teaching.**" That sentence is *falsifiable, points at one action,* and the four facts now
have a job: they're the support.

The test for a real governing message: **could a reasonable person disagree with it?** "We learned
several things about attendance" — no one can disagree, so it's not a message. "Schedule, not
quality, is the constraint" — someone could argue the opposite, so it's a claim worth defending.
If your top line survives the deletion of every bullet beneath it and still says something
decision-shaped, you've synthesized. If it collapses into "here's what we found," you've stacked.

## Practice case

Decision-maker: **you, preparing to tell yourself (and a co-teacher) what the attendance
investigation actually concluded.** From the Day-22 plan you now hold these findings:

- 6pm attendance is ~40% below the 7:30pm slot. *(confirmed)*
- Empty seats correlate with longer commute distance. *(confirmed)*
- First-time triers at 6pm return at half the rate of 7:30pm triers. *(confirmed)*
- In-room exit surveys show no complaints about difficulty or teaching. *(confirmed)*

The temptation is to report all four. Your co-teacher's time is short, and a list of four facts
will produce four opinions and no decision. Synthesize them into ONE message with three supports.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A pyramid: one governing-message sentence on top, exactly 3 supporting reasons (full sentences)
under it, each tagged with which finding backs it.

**Definition of Done:** the top line is a *disagreeable* claim (someone could argue the opposite),
not a summary of activity; the three supports are reasons, not raw facts; and the message names
the **reputation-sensitive action it implies** — recommending a schedule change to a co-teacher
who built the 6pm slot, which is a relationship you don't want to bruise with a vague "we found
some stuff."

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] My top line is ONE sentence a reasonable person could disagree with.
- [ ] It survives deleting every support below it and still says something decision-shaped.
- [ ] I have 3 (±1) supports, each a *reason*, not a restated fact.
- [ ] Each support is backed by a labeled finding.
- [ ] **What evidence, example, or counterexample would show this is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant?** (e.g., a finding that contradicts the top line and isn't accounted for.)
- [ ] I can name the action this synthesis implies.

## Common mistake to watch for

**The clean-but-useless summary.** A perfectly organized recap that lists findings without
committing to a message. It *looks* like synthesis because it's tidy, but it does the reader's
hardest work — deciding what it all means — for them, which is to say, not at all.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** the pyramid whenever you must hand a conclusion to a decision-maker. **Don't** force a
single message when the honest answer is genuinely "two things are true and they conflict" — then
the message *is* the tension, stated as one sentence ("we can fix attendance or protect the 6pm
regulars, not both cheaply").

## Reflection question

When you finished the case, did you feel safer listing all four findings than committing to one
message? That safety is the data-dump reflex — naming a message means you can be *wrong*, which
is exactly why it's worth more.

## Optional stretch

Take any analysis you've recently done in another domain (a mix decision, a client diagnosis) and
force it into one governing-message sentence plus three supports. If you can't, you haven't
finished thinking yet.
