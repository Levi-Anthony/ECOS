# Day 2 — MECE From Zero

> Week 1 · Foundations

## Learning objective

Understand MECE well enough to build a MECE list from scratch and to test any list for it.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (the MECE expense breakdown below).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

**MECE** = **M**utually **E**xclusive, **C**ollectively **E**xhaustive. It's the basic
quality test for any breakdown.

- **Mutually Exclusive:** the categories don't overlap — nothing belongs in two at once.
- **Collectively Exhaustive:** the categories cover everything — nothing real falls through.

Use **the drawer test** (from Field Manual §1): if a sock could go in two drawers, your
labels overlap (not ME); if a sock fits no drawer, your labels have a gap (not CE). A MECE set
gives every sock exactly one home.

Why it matters: overlap makes you double-count and argue in circles ("isn't that the same as
the other thing?"); gaps make you miss the real answer because it lived in a bucket nobody
drew. MECE catches both before they cost you.

The trick to *building* MECE (rather than just testing it): pick **one organizing principle**
and cut along it consistently. Mixing principles — "by region" and "by product" in the same
level — is the #1 source of overlap. One axis at a time.

## Practice case

Categorize your **monthly expenses** into a MECE set of 5–7 buckets. Then test it: take ten
actual recent purchases and try to file each into exactly one bucket. Any purchase that fits
two buckets (overlap) or none (gap) tells you to fix the categories.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A MECE expense breakdown plus the ten-purchase filing test.

**Definition of Done:** all ten test purchases file into exactly one bucket each, and your
buckets share a single organizing principle (by function, not mixed with by-vendor or
by-amount).

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] My categories use one consistent organizing principle.
- [ ] No purchase fit two categories (mutually exclusive).
- [ ] Every purchase had a home (collectively exhaustive) — an "other" bucket is ≤ ~10%.
- [ ] **What evidence, example, or counterexample would show this structure is incomplete,
      overlapping, or decision-irrelevant?** (Here: a purchase that fits two buckets or none —
      find one and fix it, or confirm none exists.)

## Common mistake to watch for

Sneaking in a **vendor or amount** as a category ("Amazon," "big purchases") alongside
function categories. Those cut along a *different* axis, so they overlap everything. If two
of your buckets answer different questions ("what kind?" vs. "from whom?"), you've mixed axes.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** the MECE test on any list you'll reason or decide from. **Don't** obsess over perfect
MECE on a throwaway brainstorm — and remember MECE is *necessary but not sufficient*: a list
can be MECE and still useless (Day 6).

## Reflection question

When you sorted purchases, did you discover an organizing principle you didn't start with?
What does that say about choosing the cut *before* you start listing?

## Optional stretch

Build a MECE breakdown of something from your work — types of clients, sources of studio
revenue, stages of a track's production — and run the filing test on real examples.
