# Day 25 — Storylining & Action Titles

> Week 4 · Synthesis & Communication

## Learning objective

Turn a recommendation into a **storyline**: write every section heading as a complete-sentence
claim (an action title), so that reading the headings *in sequence* — and nothing else — delivers
the whole argument and the recommended action.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a ~5-heading action-title storyline for the attendance recommendation).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

A normal heading is a *label*: "Attendance," "The 6pm slot," "Options." It tells the reader the
topic and makes them do the work of figuring out what you're saying about it. An **action title**
is a *claim*: a complete sentence the reader could agree or disagree with — "6pm fails on schedule
fit, not teaching quality." Labels organize; claims argue.

The storyline test is brutal and simple: **read only the headings, top to bottom.** If they tell a
coherent argument that lands on the recommendation, you have a storyline. If they read like a table
of contents ("Background. Analysis. Findings. Recommendation."), you have an outline — the reader
still has to read every paragraph to learn what you think.

The sequence is the second discipline. Action titles aren't just locally true; arranged in order
they should march: *what's the situation → what changed → what we found → therefore what to do →
how we'd know we're wrong.* Each title should make the next one feel inevitable. This is the
pyramid (Day 23) laid out horizontally as a reading path. A storyline that survives the
heading-only read is also a storyline you can *defend in two minutes* when the studio owner or TTC
board interrupts — because the spine is already the argument.

## Practice case

Decision-maker: **you, building the one-page document you'd hand a co-teacher and the studio owner
to greenlight a 6pm change** before the term schedule locks. You hold the Day-24 answer-first
recommendation (move 6pm to 7:30pm for a 4-week trial; schedule fit, not quality, is the
constraint; revert if it doesn't beat 6pm by week 3).

The trap: it's tempting to title sections by topic — "The Data," "What We Considered," "Next
Steps." Those are labels; the owner reads all of it or none of it. The constraint that forbids
"just gather more data": the owner will skim the headings in 20 seconds between classes and decide
whether to read on. Make the headings carry the verdict.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A storyline of ~5 action titles (each a complete-sentence claim), in sequence, that a reader could
follow heading-to-heading and arrive at the recommendation. Add one line under each title naming
the evidence that backs it.

**Definition of Done:** every heading is a full-sentence claim, not a topic label; read alone, the
headings tell the whole story and end on the action; and the storyline names the
**reputation-sensitive action it sets up** — circulating a written recommendation to a co-teacher
and owner with your name on the verdict, which commits you publicly to a position you'll be held to
if the trial fails.

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] Every heading is a complete sentence making a claim, not a topic label.
- [ ] Reading headings only, the argument is coherent and lands on the recommendation.
- [ ] The titles are in an order where each makes the next feel inevitable.
- [ ] Each title is backed by a named piece of evidence.
- [ ] **What evidence, example, or counterexample would show this is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant?** (e.g., a heading the reader could agree with no matter what you found.)
- [ ] I can name the action this storyline commits me to.

## Common mistake to watch for

**The table-of-contents storyline.** Headings like "Background / Analysis / Recommendation" pass
for structure but carry no argument — they're labels wearing the costume of claims. If a heading
would be equally true for a *different* investigation, it's a label. Rewrite it until it could only
title *this* document.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** action titles for any document meant to persuade or decide — board memos, IT proposals,
ECTango operating decisions. **Don't** force claim-headings onto reference material (a syllabus, a
runbook) where the reader wants to *find* a topic, not be argued at; there, labels are correct.

## Reflection question

Did your headings end up making claims you'd actually defend, or did you soften them into safe
labels the moment you wrote them down? The softening is the same flinch as the data-dump reflex —
a claim can be wrong, a label can't.

## Optional stretch

Take the last multi-section thing you wrote (a class plan, a client email, a release note) and
rewrite every heading as a claim. Then read the headings alone. Did the document have an argument,
or were you just moving topics around?
