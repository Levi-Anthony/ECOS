# Day 14 — Week 2 Integration & Checkpoint (Bridge to Week 3)

> Week 2 · Practice · *load-bearing · checkpoint · correction loop · Week-3 bridge*

## Learning objective

Pick the right cut type for contrasting problems — and, for a problem where two lenses are
plausible, explain *why one is a weaker starting point than the other.*

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (choose and build a cut for the two-lens case below).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

Week 2 gave you a toolkit: profit trees, segmentation, process maps, root-cause, decision
factors, strategy options. The risk now is **fake competence** — you can build any of them, so
you build the one you like best instead of the one the problem needs. Today tests *selection*,
not construction.

The harder skill arrives in the two-lens case. Real problems often admit two plausible
structures. The beginner picks one and defends it; the skilled thinker notices both and asks
*which is the better starting point, and what would make me switch.* That comparison — "I'd
start with X because it localizes the problem faster; I'd switch to Y if the cause turns out to
be self-reinforcing" — is the exact muscle Week 3 trains. Today is the bridge: you stop asking
"can I use this tool?" and start asking **"which tool first, and when do I switch?"**

## Practice case

Three short problems. For each, name the best cut/lens and justify in one sentence:
1. **Operational:** "Orders ship late about a third of the time." *(Where does it break?)*
2. **Strategic:** "We have budget to grow one program next year — which?" *(How to choose?)*
3. **Two-lens-plausible:** "Clients churn after their first 90 days." A *process map* (find
   where trust breaks) and a *causal loop* (churn that compounds) are both defensible. Pick your
   **starting** lens, justify it, and name the finding that would make you switch.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A lens choice + one-line justification for each of the three problems, plus a built structure
for the two-lens case and a stated switch condition.

**Definition of Done:** each choice is justified by a *feature of the problem* (not habit); for
the two-lens case you can articulate why your starting lens beats the alternative *and* what
evidence would flip you to the other.

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] Each lens choice is justified by a feature of the problem, not preference.
- [ ] For the two-lens case I named why my starting lens beats the alternative.
- [ ] I stated a concrete switch condition ("I'd move to the other lens if…").
- [ ] **What evidence would show my chosen starting lens is the wrong one?** (Name it.)
- [ ] I can state the decision each structure improves.

## Common mistake to watch for

Defaulting to the lens you're best at. If you chose a process map for all three, or an issue
tree for all three, you're constructing, not selecting. The tell: your justifications all sound
the same.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** deliberate lens-selection on any problem big enough to matter. **Don't** over-deliberate
a tiny problem — but notice that the two-lens *comparison* is cheap and almost always sharpens
your thinking, even when you end up with your first guess.

## External / AI Review
Paste a deliverable (no private details) into an AI or show a peer. Ask for:
1. Overlap/gap check. 2. Decision-relevance check. 3. Hidden-assumption check.
4. One stronger alternative structure. 5. What evidence would change the recommendation.
6. Whether the structure is useful or merely tidy.
