# Day 12 — Everyday Decision Decomposition

> Week 2 · Practice

## Learning objective

Break a real personal/operational decision into a small set of **weighted factors** so the
trade-off becomes explicit — and the structure, not the mood of the moment, names the call.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a weighted-factor table or decision tree for the practice decision).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

Most everyday decisions are made by **vibe collapse**: a tangle of considerations resolves into a
gut "yes" or "no," and the reasoning evaporates. Decomposition's job here isn't to override your
gut — it's to make the trade-off *legible* so you can check whether the gut is tracking the factors
that actually matter or just the loudest one.

The move: list the 3–6 factors that genuinely bear on the decision, **weight** them by how much
they matter (not how easy they are to measure), score each option against each factor, and read the
result. A weighted-factor table is the lightweight form; a **decision tree** (Template 5) is the
form when there's real uncertainty with payoffs you can estimate — branch by option, then by
uncertain outcome, and compute expected value.

Two failure modes the structure exposes. First, the **invisible heavy factor**: the thing that's
actually driving you (fear of a hard conversation, a sunk cost) that you'd never list because it's
embarrassing — name it or the table lies. Second, **false precision**: assigning weights to three
decimal places on guesses. The structure's value is *forcing the trade-off into the open*, not
manufacturing a number. If the table and your gut disagree, that disagreement is the finding — sit
with it rather than trusting either blindly.

## Practice case

**You, deciding whether to take a paid corporate-event gig.** A downtown hotel offers $650 for two
hours of tango performance-plus-mini-lesson at a company party in three weeks — on a Friday night.
The tension: Friday is your regular ECTango social, and you'd have to cancel or hand it off. Messy
details: $650 is good money and you're saving for new recording gear; the social runs at a small
profit but it's the heart of your community-building and your beginner pipeline; the hotel hints at
"more events like this" but won't commit; a sub could cover the social but the regulars notice when
you're gone. **Distractor:** the dangled "more events" — easy to overweight a vague maybe.
**Plausible-but-wrong lens:** treating it as pure revenue (Day 8 thinking) — $650 > social profit,
so take it — which ignores the pipeline and community factors that don't show up in this month's
cash. **Why it's not obvious:** the costs are diffuse and delayed (a thinned social three weeks out,
a regular who drifts) while the $650 is concrete and immediate. **Constraint:** you must answer the
hotel within 48 hours — no time to test-run a sub or survey the regulars first.

Decompose it into weighted factors (or a decision tree), make the trade-off explicit, and call it.

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A weighted-factor table (3–6 factors, weights, per-option scores) **or** a decision tree (Template 5)
with EV, ending in a stated call and a one-line note on what the structure surfaced that your gut had
buried.

**Definition of Done:** the factors are weighted by importance, not measurability; at least one
"invisible heavy factor" (community/pipeline/sunk-cost) is named explicitly; you can state **which
factor would actually flip the decision if it changed** and **which factor is tidy but probably
low-value** here (e.g., the vague "more events" maybe); and you can name the decision it improves —
the 48-hour yes/no to the hotel.

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] My factors are weighted by how much they matter, not how easy they are to measure.
- [ ] I named at least one factor my gut was hiding (community, pipeline, sunk cost, conflict-avoidance).
- [ ] I avoided false precision — weights reflect real judgment, not invented decimals.
- [ ] **Which factor would actually flip the decision — and which factor is tidy but probably low-value?**
- [ ] **What evidence, example, or counterexample would show this structure is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant?**
- [ ] I can state the decision this structure improves and what the structure surfaced that the gut buried.

## Common mistake to watch for

**Letting one loud factor masquerade as the whole decision** — usually the immediate, concrete,
measurable one ($650) crowding out the diffuse, delayed, important ones (pipeline, community). The
weighting step exists precisely to demote the loud factor to its true size.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** when a decision has competing factors you can name and a genuine trade-off (not a no-brainer).
**Don't** over-build it for trivial or fully-reversible choices — a five-factor table for "what to eat"
is procrastination dressed as rigor. And don't trust the table over a strong gut objection; the
disagreement means a factor is missing or mis-weighted (revisit, don't override).

## Reflection question

When your weighted table and your gut disagreed, which did you trust — and was the gut tracking a real
factor you'd left off the table, or just the loudest, most immediate one?
