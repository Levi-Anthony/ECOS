# Day 20 — Stakeholder Mapping & Incentives

> Week 3 · Lens Selection & Sequencing

## Learning objective

Map the actors in a people-driven problem on a power/interest grid AND — the load-bearing move —
name what each one is actually *rewarded* for, so the structure surfaces incentives, not just
categories.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a stakeholder + incentive map of the spine case).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

When an outcome depends on what people *choose to do*, no driver tree or causal loop will save
you — you need a **stakeholder map**. The familiar version is the power/interest grid: place each
actor by how much influence they have and how much they care, so you know whom to satisfy, whom
to keep informed, and whom to manage closely.

But the grid is only the skeleton. The bad habit — and it's the one to break today — is
**mistaking categories for incentives.** Knowing someone is a "high-power, high-interest
stakeholder" tells you *where they sit*, not *why they act*. The real lever is the second column:
**what is each actor rewarded for?** A venue isn't "interested in attendance" in the abstract —
it's rewarded for *predictable* headcount that fills its calendar and bar. Instructors aren't
"interested in the class" — they may be rewarded for low-friction weeks, which is exactly why
"schedule fatigue" is their preferred diagnosis. Beginners are rewarded by feeling progress;
regulars by room energy and challenge. Until you write the incentive next to each box, you have a
seating chart, not a map of how the system will move. People follow incentives, not categories.

## Practice case — the Week-3 spine

**ECTango attendance is inconsistent.** Beginner attendance is up after social posts, but repeat
attendance is down; advanced dancers say class energy is lower; the venue wants a predictable
weekly headcount; Levi suspects curriculum sequencing, instructors blame schedule fatigue.

Map the actors: **beginners**, **regulars/advanced dancers**, **instructors**, **the venue**, and
**Levi** himself. For each, fill two things: (1) power × interest position, and (2) the incentive
— *what they are actually rewarded for.* Then read the conflicts straight off the map: the venue
is rewarded for predictable volume, instructors for low-friction weeks, so both quietly resist a
curriculum re-sequence that adds short-term churn — even though that may be the real fix. You
can't convene everyone for a meeting, so the map must let you predict reactions, not poll them.

Sequencing questions: **what does the stakeholder/incentive lens reveal** (who will resist or
champion each intervention, and *why* the instructors' "fatigue" framing serves their incentive)
and **what does it hide** (the mechanics — it won't tell you whether the curriculum actually
frustrates beginners, only who'd block fixing it)? **What evidence would make you switch** back to
a causal loop or decision tree? **What second lens follows** to pressure-test the chosen fix?

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A stakeholder map of the spine case's five actors, each placed on power/interest **and** annotated
with its specific incentive, plus a one-line read of the sharpest incentive conflict.

**Definition of Done:** every actor has both a grid position and a concrete incentive (not a
category restated); you named ≥1 conflict where an actor's incentive opposes the likely-best fix;
and you can state **the decision it improves** — which intervention to frame in whose terms, or
whom to win over first — plus what the lens hides (the underlying mechanics of the problem).

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] Every actor has a power/interest position *and* a concrete incentive written next to it.
- [ ] At least one incentive is something the actor is *rewarded for*, not a restated category.
- [ ] I named a conflict where an incentive opposes the fix the diagnosis points to.
- [ ] I read the instructors' "schedule fatigue" claim against their incentive, not at face value.
- [ ] **What evidence would show this structure is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant — or that I should switch lenses?**
- [ ] I can state the decision this improves and what the stakeholder lens hides.

## Common mistake to watch for

**Mistaking categories for incentives.** Labeling everyone by quadrant ("high power / low
interest") feels like analysis but predicts nothing — two actors in the same box can pull in
opposite directions. The tell: your map has positions but no "rewarded for" column, so it can't
explain why anyone would resist the obvious fix.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** a stakeholder/incentive map when the outcome turns on what people decide to do, and
especially when a "rational" fix keeps mysteriously stalling. **Do NOT** use it when the problem
is mechanical or numeric and people's choices aren't the binding constraint — there a driver tree
or process map is sharper. On the spine case, the stakeholder lens is the *wrong* first move: run
it before diagnosing the mechanics and you'll manage politics around a problem you haven't located.

## Reflection question

When you wrote the instructors' incentive, did "schedule fatigue" start looking less like a neutral
diagnosis and more like a convenient one? Notice how naming the reward reframed a claim you'd been
taking at face value. That re-read is the whole point of the incentive column.
