# Day 15 — Hypothesis-Driven Analysis & Hypothesis Trees

> Week 3 · Lens Selection & Sequencing

## Learning objective

Lead with your best-guess *answer*, decompose it into the claims that must all be true, and
design tests that try to *disprove* it fast — not confirm it.

## Bridge exercise (3 minutes)

Pull the **Day-14 two-lens case** ("Clients churn after their first 90 days"). In one sentence,
write *why the lens you picked there is only a starting point, not the answer.* A lens tells you
where to look; it does not tell you what's true. Today you hold that humility while doing the
opposite move — committing to a guess on purpose, so you can kill it quickly.

## Minimum Viable Rep — 25 minutes
1. Produce the structure (a hypothesis tree on the spine case, Template 2).
2. Score it using the rubric.
3. Name one defect.
4. Repair one branch.
5. Write one sentence: "This structure helps because…"

## Concept lesson

A **hypothesis tree** inverts the usual order. Instead of mapping the whole space and waiting for
the answer to emerge, you state your *best guess at the answer first*, then list the sub-claims
that must **all** be true for it to hold. Under each claim you note the single observation that
would confirm or kill it. The structure exists to find the *fastest disproof*, not to decorate
the hunch.

This is the lens for "I have a suspicion and limited time." Its power is speed: you test the
load-bearing claim first, and if it fails, the whole hypothesis collapses and you've saved days.
Its danger is the exact opposite habit — **confirmation bias**. A hypothesis tree run badly
becomes a machine for proving your first guess: you list claims you already believe, run tests
designed to pass, and ignore the disconfirming reading. The discipline is to write a real **kill
criterion** — the one finding that would make you abandon the hypothesis — and to go looking for
it first. If you can't name what would change your mind, you're not testing, you're rehearsing.

## Practice case — the Week-3 spine

**ECTango attendance is inconsistent.** Beginner attendance is up after social posts, but repeat
attendance is down; advanced dancers say class energy is lower; the venue wants a predictable
weekly headcount; Levi suspects curriculum sequencing, instructors blame schedule fatigue.

Pick your strongest single hypothesis (e.g., *"Repeat attendance is down because the curriculum
sequence frustrates beginners by week three"*). Build the tree: list the 3 claims that must all
be true, the test for each, and a kill criterion. You can't run a survey this week — so design
tests against attendance logs and observation you already have.

Then answer the sequencing questions: **what does the hypothesis lens reveal** (a fast, falsifiable
path to one cause) **and what does it hide** (rival causes you didn't guess, and the feedback the
instructors' "fatigue" theory might point to)? **What evidence would make you switch** to a driver
tree or causal loop? **What second lens might follow?**

## Deliverable + Definition of Done

A filled Template-2 hypothesis tree on the spine case: stated answer, ≥3 must-be-true claims,
a test per claim, and an explicit kill criterion.

**Definition of Done:** the tree leads with an answer (not a question); at least one claim is
load-bearing (if it fails the hypothesis dies); the kill criterion is a real finding you could
actually observe; and you can name **the decision it improves** — which single intervention to
test next — plus what this lens hides (rival hypotheses, feedback).

## Self-check rubric

- [ ] My tree states a specific *answer*, not a question to explore.
- [ ] Every claim has a test that could *disconfirm* it, not just confirm it.
- [ ] I wrote a real kill criterion I could actually observe this week.
- [ ] I named which claim is load-bearing (its failure kills the whole hypothesis).
- [ ] **What evidence would show this structure is incomplete, overlapping, or decision-irrelevant — or that I should switch lenses?**
- [ ] I can state the intervention this points to and what the lens hides.

## Common mistake to watch for

**Using the tree to confirm your first guess.** The tell: every test is phrased to pass, there's
no kill criterion, and disconfirming readings get explained away. A hypothesis tree that can't be
killed isn't analysis — it's advocacy for the hunch you walked in with.

## When to use / when NOT to use

**Use** a hypothesis tree when you have a credible hunch and little time, and you genuinely want to
be proven wrong fast. **Do NOT** use it when you have no real hunch yet (you'll invent one and
anchor on it), or when you know you'd ignore disconfirming evidence — there, map the space with an
issue tree or a causal loop first. On the spine case, a hypothesis tree is the *wrong* first lens
if the real story is the feedback between turnout, energy, and retention (Day 17's territory).

## Reflection question

When you wrote the kill criterion, did you feel resistance — a quiet hope it wouldn't trigger?
That flinch is confirmation bias announcing itself. Naming it is how you stop it.
